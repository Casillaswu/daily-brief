01 · TOP STORY Regulation ○Neutral N. America · Prediction Markets

The prediction-market lawsuits produce verdicts: CFTC loses to Wisconsin's state government, New York sues Kalshi for up to $36B

Bloomberg Tax · CNBC · CNN · 07/29–07/31

Prediction markets are platforms that let people bet on whether an event will happen — including who wins a match — and the biggest operator is Kalshi, which holds a license from the US federal futures regulator, the CFTC. The dispute: is this gambling (state governments' turf) or a financial product (federal turf)?

The federal side lost. On July 29 a federal judge denied the CFTC's bid to block Wisconsin's state government from enforcing. The reasoning: registering with the CFTC does not automatically exempt you from a state government's gambling law, and Wisconsin's law already covers this kind of betting. Kalshi's "a federal license overrides state law" argument was rejected head-on — there is still room to appeal. On July 31 New York's governor and attorney general sued Kalshi as illegal gambling, on the grounds that it operates without a New York license, and asked the court for a restraining order, with damages computed against its earnings all the way up to $36 billion. Kalshi's reply: "political theater."


Bottom line: for licensees that dutifully hold state licenses and pay tax, short-term this is good; but the federal appeal is not over, so we mark it Neutral. One number: as of this week, state governments and prediction-market platforms have clashed in court 29 times and states have won 23 — roughly a 79% win rate; the route of "one higher-level license blocks lower-level regulation" is actually losing in the US. Action: for markets sensitive to offshore platforms like the Philippines and Vietnam, remember this playbook — once local licensees find a new platform poaching their tax base, they push the government to act on "illegal gambling, fine it, block it," which is faster and harsher than a consumer-protection angle. If you have deals with prediction-market platforms, write "who bears the loss if a jurisdiction bans it" into the contract now.

SIGNAL 02 · GLOBAL · OPERATORS

BetMGM's earnings admit it for the first time: prediction markets hurt results, the $500M profit target is pushed back

BetMGM (the North American operator jointly owned by MGM and Entain) reported Q2 on July 28. Net revenue $711M, up just 3% year-on-year; online casino (slots, table games) grew 8%, online sports was nearly flat. The key line: it pushed its "$500M EBITDA" target back from 2027, with the stated reason "prediction market regulatory complexity." Retail betting revenue was near zero this quarter, half because customers won too much, half because it had to spend more to reward players so they would not be lured away by prediction markets.


Bottom line: bad news for traditional operators caught in the middle, little impact on cash-rich groups that can absorb it. The market used to only guess; this time the operator admits it. Prediction markets need no identity verification and have no deposit cap, poaching exactly the customers who find traditional platforms a hassle. Action: Flutter reports August 5 and DraftKings August 6 — use BetMGM as the yardstick; if both also admit it, "building your own prediction market loses money short-term" is industry common sense. SIGNAL 03 · ASIA · MACAU

Macau July revenue down 8.4% y-o-y: up 9.4% from June; two typhoons and the World Cup dragged it

Macau reported July gross gaming revenue (total player losses) of MOP$20.3B (US$2.51B) on August 1 — down 8.4% year-on-year, but up 9.4% from June, and the first seven months are still up 4.4% versus last year. Two reasons for July's weakness: the World Cup running to the July 19 final diverted premium-segment betting money; and a typhoon at the start and end of the month (Maysak, Noul), the latter disrupting the airport and transport to and from Hong Kong.


Bottom line: neutral for Macau casinos. Within July's negative number, the World Cup and typhoons are one-offs, and the month-on-month rebound shows the base is intact. Watch August: with the one-offs gone, if it is still down double digits, the problem is not weather or football but genuinely fewer customers. Macau's H1 gaming tax reached only 55% of the annual target; if it isn't recouped, the government's funding pressure turns into tighter operating demands on casinos. Action: for those serving Macau's premium segment, August daily GGR is the dividing line — don't be spooked by a single negative month, and don't rush to be optimistic either. Read original →

SIGNAL 04 · ASIA · PHILIPPINES

PAGCOR blinks again: the 8/1 decommission was scrapped; the accreditation deadline is pushed to September 30

The Philippine gaming regulator PAGCOR (which both licenses private operators and runs its own Casino Filipino) had set a July 31 accreditation deadline for content and tech suppliers, with uncertified systems, platforms and games to be decommissioned from August 1. But on July 20 its Electronic Gaming Licensing Department issued a memo (approved by the board on July 16) pushing the deadline to September 30 and moving decommissioning to October 1, citing "widespread procedural delays" in getting accredited. In other words, nobody was decommissioned on August 1.


Bottom line: this is not "the regulator means what it says" — it is PAGCOR going soft once more under procedural pressure. For accredited licensees with a local footprint, two extra months of buffer, neutral-to-good; for offshore suppliers not yet done, another two-month reprieve. But don't treat the extension as nothing — it locks in "action from October 1," and Gaming System Administrators (GSAs) that use uncertified suppliers face sanctions too. Action: re-plan accreditation against September 30, don't bet on a third delay, and confirm whether every supplier on your platform can pass by September 30. Read original →

SIGNAL 05 · PRODUCT · B2B

Prediction markets become "included in the package": DATA.BET builds them into its white-label platform, so mid-size operators can offer them too

DATA.BET (a gambling data and tech supplier) announced on July 30 that operators on its white-label platform can now offer prediction-market betting to players directly. Previously only big companies that spent heavily to build their own — DraftKings, FanDuel, Kalshi — could; now the supplier has made it a ready-made white-label module, so mid-size operators without build capacity can launch fast.


Bottom line: good for mid-size brands built on white-label with no budget to build, bad for big companies that sold "only we have it" — once the feature becomes off-the-shelf that anyone can buy, the scarcity is gone. But one caveat: prediction markets' legality is still in court in many places, and the supplier giving you the feature does not carry the operator's compliance risk. Action: white-label brands can move "should we add prediction markets" from "can't" to "let's evaluate this year," but understand the target market's stance on this kind of betting before launching. Regulation○Neutral📌 Editor's Note

When deemed "illegal," the fine is your entire revenue — a lesson emerging-market operators should note first

📍 Editorial synthesis from this issue's Signal 01 and §1 — no single original link

To assess a new platform's risk in a market, don't just look at "is there legislative discussion," look at "if it is deemed illegal, how are damages computed." The US answer this week — against total revenue, into the tens of billions. This logic applies to any market; only the opponent changes from prediction markets to offshore platforms.


Bottom line: good for licensees that pay tax by the book, bad for offshore and gray-area plays. Action: if you carry offshore-related risk, price in "if deemed illegal, damages are computed against total revenue" — don't assume the worst case is one fine and a takedown. When this line lights up, move risk toward a compliant structure.

M&A & Capital
Operators○NeutralMarket structure

June prediction-market volume is already 85% of sports betting: too big to skip, and building your own loses money first

Casino.org · Morgan Stanley · 07/30

A comparison: in June alone, prediction-market volume topped $40B and actual player spend was about $10B, while the whole online sports-betting handle for the period was about $12B — prediction-market consumer spend is already about 85% of sports betting. It is long past a niche experiment; it is a mainstream category approaching sports betting in size. And precisely because it is too big to skip, operators are forced to pour money in and then lose money for a stretch (BetMGM's delayed profit target is the living example).


Bottom line: good for big companies that can afford to build and absorb early losses, bad for those with a single sportsbook line. Action: in the August 5 Flutter and August 6 DraftKings earnings, how much profit the prediction-market spend eats is the most concrete data for whether building your own is worth it — wait for those two numbers before deciding.

Asia

The Philippines (Signal 04) and Macau (Signal 03) were covered above; here is one takeaway worth keeping.

Market○Neutral📌 Editor's Note

PAGCOR's two delays, a reminder: don't treat a regulator's deadline as fixed, but the direction won't change

📍 Editorial synthesis from this issue's Signal 04 and §5 — no single original link

The Philippine B2B accreditation deadline has slipped from July 31 all the way to September 30 — the second time it has been pushed back. Two reminders for operators in Asia. One: a regulator like PAGCOR that issues its own licenses and also runs its own casinos sets hard deadlines but, when they arrive, often relents citing "procedural delays" — treating its dates as fixed for production and media planning gets you disrupted. Two: an extension is not surrender — the uncertified will still be cleared eventually, only later; the direction hasn't changed.


Bottom line: good for licensees with a local anchor preparing for the eventual direction (extra buffer), bad for those betting "it will keep delaying, so why bother" — the October 1 cut lands sooner or later. Action: do the accreditation anyway, planned against September 30; but don't stake product and media timing on "some early deadline definitely clears the field" — leave room for a delay.

LatAm — Brazil Focus

LatAm · Brazil
Regulation○NeutralBrazil · Authorization rules

Brazil starts rewriting the rules for "how operators get commercial authorization": a 45-day consultation opened July 27

Bright Side of News · 07/27

Brazil's betting regulator SPA (under the Ministry of Finance) opened a 45-day public consultation from July 27, closing September 9, to redefine how fixed-odds operators obtain "commercial authorization." Brazil currently has 87 licensed operators and 14 in the queue. This step follows July's wave of ad rules, and the direction is clear: raise the entry barrier notch by notch.


Bottom line: good for big brands that have spent on compliance and hold a license, bad for smaller brands hoping to enter Brazil cheaply. This changes not ads or tax but "the rule for getting authorization itself" — the definition of the entry ticket. Action: for Brazil operators, over these 45 days watch the draft's new requirements for authorization applications; it directly decides whether next year you can operate under the current structure. Licensees should treat this as a window to voice opinions, not just watch.

Sports○Neutral📌 Editor's Note

The World Cup is over, but licensees' hard problem is unchanged: how to make betting as frictionless as prediction markets without breaking the rules

📍 Editorial synthesis from this issue's Signal 02, §2 and §4 — no single original link

Prediction markets need no identity verification and have no deposit cap, and June volume already hit 85% of sports-betting handle, vacuuming up the customers who find traditional platforms a hassle. This week's BetMGM earnings put the first price on the problem — to defend, traditional operators must spend more to retain players and push profit targets back. This is licensees' hardest problem for the next two years: keep compliance, make betting as frictionless as prediction markets, and absorb the early losses of building your own product.
