01 · LEAD Operators −Negative N.America · Q2 earnings

US Q2 earnings week confirms it: prediction markets are eating sports-betting handle — Flutter swings to a loss, DraftKings profit halved

GlobeNewswire · iGaming Business · CasinoBeats · 08/05–08/07

Two US sports-betting leaders reported this week and, for the first time, named prediction markets (PMs — platforms that let people bet on whether an event will happen, including who wins a game) as a primary drag.

Flutter (FanDuel's parent, listed in London) on Aug 5: revenue of US$4.326bn, up 3% YoY, but EPS swung to a US$1.57 loss and adjusted EBITDA fell 45% to US$508m. Three things stacked up — softer US sports-betting revenue, higher UK online gambling tax, and heavy World Cup marketing. Its own prediction-market unit earned just US$6m in Q2, and analysts said outright that FanDuel is roughly 9–12 months behind DraftKings in prediction markets. DraftKings (a US sports-betting leader, top-two by share) on Aug 6: revenue down 5% YoY, adjusted EBITDA halved from US$300.6m a year earlier to US$114.64m, though it reaffirmed full-year revenue guidance of US$6.5–6.9bn. One number to size it up: Bank of America (BofA) estimates June consumer wagering on prediction markets at roughly US$10bn — about 85% of US online sports-betting handle over the same period.


Bottom line: this is not a one-off bad quarter, it is structural. For operators that only run online sports betting, whose moat is just "a legal license + brand," this is −EV — player money is flowing toward something with an unsettled regulatory identity but a lower-friction betting experience. Execution: in markets built on sports betting like the Philippines and Vietnam, stop assuming "handle only goes up." This "bet on whether an event happens" product form will eventually arrive in local or offshore versions and pull volume — treat it as a direct competitor to study now, not a fringe topic. For any partnership tied to a pure sportsbook, re-run its 12-month volume outlook.

SIGNAL 02 · N.AMERICA · REGULATION

CFTC orders prediction markets to drop American-style odds, Kalshi complies: same week, Iowa hearing, Wisconsin appeal and Arizona criminal charges all move at once

The US federal Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) on Aug 8 told prediction-market platforms to strip "+150 / −200" American moneyline odds from contract pages, arguing the presentation dresses financial contracts up to look like sports betting and is misleading; Kalshi (a CFTC-licensed prediction market) confirmed it will comply. Three litigation fronts moved the same week: Kalshi's case against the Iowa state government went to a hearing, with the veteran gaming-industry body AGA joining on the state's side; the CFTC appealed its Wisconsin defeat; and Arizona brought criminal charges against Kalshi while the CFTC counter-sued Arizona for obstructing its oversight.


Bottom line: short-term favorable for licensed operators that dutifully hold state licenses and pay tax — the claim that "a single federal license overrides state gambling law" is being questioned court by court; but the federal appeal is not over and cases are spread across multiple circuits, so Neutral. Execution: the playbook — "the moment a local licensee sees a platform taking its tax, it pushes government to move via the 'illegal gambling, fine it, block it' angle" — applies in the Philippines, Vietnam and Brazil. If you have a partnership with a prediction-market platform, write "who eats the loss if it's banned" into the contract now. Read original →

SIGNAL 03 · N.AMERICA · PRODUCT

DraftKings goes on the offensive: no longer defending — building its own prediction-market product

DraftKings' stance on its earnings call was the strategic move most worth watching this week: rather than just guarding a sportsbook getting bitten, it will build its own prediction-market product and compete directly — effectively conceding this product form won't disappear and can only be met by entering it.


Bottom line: this is the turn from "getting bitten" to "getting in." Once licensed, compliance-staffed majors like DraftKings and FanDuel all build these, it endorses the product form and washes half the "prediction markets = grey offshore" stigma away. For people making sports-betting content and plans, it's a change signal — over the next 12 months, "prediction-market-style betting" moves from the fringe to a line on the mainstream operator menu. Execution: start studying its product shape now (how contracts are designed, how settlement is explained, how marketing sidesteps "this is gambling") — don't wait until it becomes standard to catch up. SIGNAL 05 · LATAM · BRAZIL

Brazil H1 betting tax tops €1.15bn, rate up to 13% and 15% by 2028; SPA orders licensees to bring sites into compliance within August

Brazil's regulated betting market paid more than €1.15bn in federal tax in H1. On rates: from January 2026 the gross-gaming-revenue tax on licensees rose from 12% to 13%, and the law already schedules a rise to 15% before 2028. On enforcement: the SPA (Secretaria de Prêmios e Apostas, Brazil's Ministry of Finance betting regulator) requires licensees to bring websites and apps into line with the new rules within August, or be fined. The SECAP portal currently lists 87 operators holding valid Bets ANGB licenses.


Bottom line: Brazil's path is clear — use tax numbers to prove "regulation works" (H1's €1.15bn is a political chip) while steadily raising rates and compliance bars. For licensed operators, costs get heavier each year, −EV; but for the market overall this moves toward "stable and predictable," +EV for long-term investment. Execution: model Brazil costs at a 15% tax rate, not today's 13%; reserve budget for "compliance re-versioning at any time" — the SPA's "fix by the deadline or be fined" cadence will become routine. Regulation○Neutral📌 Editor's Pick

Federal losing in court while writing its own rules: two tracks to read separately

📍 Editorial synthesis from Signal 02 / §1 — no single source link

The US fight is running on two tracks at once. In court: the CFTC argues "a federal license overrides state law" for platforms like Kalshi, yet keeps losing — from Wisconsin onward. In rulemaking: the CFTC is drafting its own "event contract" rules, with the public-comment window just closed on 1,400+ submissions. The courts decide "does the current setup count"; the rules decide "how the future setup should be governed" — and the two may land on opposite conclusions.


Bottom line: for anyone reading the situation, keep these two tracks apart. Even if the CFTC keeps losing in court, the rules it writes could still open a legal lane for prediction markets — only after a year or two, and not necessarily overriding state enforcement. Execution: to judge whether prediction markets can operate long-term in the US, don't look only at who won this week's case — track what the CFTC's final rules look like too; until both tracks are clear, keep an exit on any US-related product commitment.

North America

US operator earnings already got full cards in Signal 01 and §4; here is the market-structure observation.

Operators○Neutral📌 Editor's Pick

Prediction-market handle already ~85% of sports betting: it is no longer a complement, but a substitute

📍 Editorial synthesis from Signal 01 / §4 — representative source below

Back to that scale figure: BofA estimates June consumer wagering on prediction markets at roughly US$10bn, about 85% of US online sports-betting handle. A year ago it was treated as a niche experiment, a sports-betting add-on; now its size rivals sports betting itself, and its nature shifts accordingly — from "one more option" to "the same money moving between the two." The three leaders' Q2 reports put a price on that shift: whichever side the money flows to is the side whose report looks good.


Bottom line: whether a product is a "complement" or a "substitute" comes down to whether it draws new demand or existing demand. Prediction markets draw existing demand — the same sports-betting players, the same budget — so it is zero-sum for sports betting. Execution: when sizing a market, stop adding prediction markets as "extra volume on top of sports betting"; treat it as a deduction that "siphons off existing sports-betting handle," so the sports-betting ceiling you arrive at isn't inflated.

Sports−Negative📌 Editor's Pick

The prediction-market threat to sportsbooks is fundamentally repackaging "betting" from 'gambling' into 'trading'

📍 Editorial synthesis from Signal 01 / 02 / 03 — no single source link

The biggest external variable for sports betting this year isn't another sportsbook — it's prediction markets. Their punch is in the narrative: recasting "betting on a game" as "buying an event contract," sidestepping the psychological barrier of "this is gambling" and part of the regulation. The CFTC's demand this week to drop American odds (see Signal 02) is exactly an attempt to cut off that "looks-like-gambling" packaging.


Bottom line: whoever gets to define "is this gambling or trading" controls this market's regulatory home and tax burden — the core battleground for sports betting over the next two to three years; for traditional sportsbooks, −EV (one more rival that dodges regulation). Execution: to fight back, sportsbook brands need to (1) add "event-betting" mechanics on the product side, and (2) hold the "we're licensed, taxed, trustworthy" position in the narrative — turning prediction markets' "regulation unsettled" into your own trust advantage.
