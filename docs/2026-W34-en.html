01 · Top Story Regulation −Negative APAC · Australia

Australia writes a full set of advertising rules into law: betting ads banned from 15 minutes before to 5 minutes after live events, athlete and influencer endorsements banned, inducements banned for 14 days after sign-up

Inside Asian Gaming · SBC News · 08/21

Australia's parliament passed the Interactive Gambling Amendment (Gambling Reform) Act 2026 on 19 August, and the government announced it on 20 August. This is not a blanket ban; it is a detailed set of rules covering how betting is marketed, taking effect 1 January 2027. On advertising: broadcasters and online content services carrying live sport may not run betting ads between 5:00am and 8:30pm from 15 minutes before an event starts to 5 minutes after it ends; in-venue advertising is banned, and gambling brands may not appear on player or referee kit; athletes, celebrities and influencers may not be used to promote betting; television betting ads are capped at three per hour in that same window; radio ads are banned during the school run; broadcasting odds promotions is prohibited. Online platforms may only serve betting ads to users who are logged in, aged 18 or over, and given an opt-out.

On inducements: direct marketing inducements to new customers are banned for 14 days after registration; banned for three months after a person deregisters from the national self-exclusion register BetStop; and banned entirely for any customer assessed as high risk. Gambling companies are also barred from paying staff commission based on customer activity levels. The package includes a national betting-ad opt-out register, powers for banks and payment systems to block transactions to unlicensed operators, wider site-blocking authority for the Australian Communications and Media Authority (ACMA), and bans on online keno and offshore-run lotteries based on other draws. Two cost-recovery levy acts passed the same week, pushing the cost of enforcing the new rules onto gambling companies. Communications Minister Anika Wells called it the strongest set of gambling rules in Australia's history.


Bottom line: what matters here is not how strict the act is but how specifically it names every part of marketing execution — broadcast windows, ads per hour, who may endorse, when inducements may be sent, how staff are paid. Rules like these usually sit in voluntary codes or licence conditions, which leaves a lot of grey area. Once they are in statute, the grey area is gone. -EV for gambling brands that acquire through live-sport slots and influencer endorsement, because both routes are effectively closed in Australia; +EV for brands that have already moved budget into owned content, organic social and retention-led CRM, because competitors are now forced to fight in the same pool. In practice: this set of rules works as a checklist to run against current marketing practice in the Philippines and Brazil — Senate Bill 2347 now moving through the Philippine congress (see Signal 02) and the state-level advertising limits rolling out across Brazil target exactly the same list of items. Anything already banned in Australia belongs on a 12-month replacement plan; waiting for local rules to land before looking for alternatives leaves no time to build them.

SIGNAL 02 · ASIA · PHILIPPINES

Arden Consult: more than 60% of licensed Philippine online operators now sit below PAGCOR's new floor, with existing licences quoted at US$3m to US$15m

A regulatory guide published on 20 August by Arden Consult, a Philippine legal and regulatory advisory firm, finds that at second-quarter revenue run rates, more than 60% of licensed gaming system administrators (GSAs) fall below the minimum revenue floor set by PAGCOR (the Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation). The mechanic: a GSA offering electronic casino games pays the higher of a percentage regulatory fee or a minimum guaranteed fee (MGF), with the MGF set at PHP 9 million per month (roughly US$147,000, about NT$4.6 million), which corresponds to a minimum monthly gross gaming revenue benchmark of PHP 30 million. GSAs without electronic casino games pay a minimum of PHP 3 million per month. Phase one started 1 July and runs to the end of 2026; from January 2027 the benchmark rises to PHP 35 million and the MGF to PHP 10.5 million. The floor is fixed — operators pay it even when actual revenue falls short. Arden estimates Philippine online gross gaming revenue at roughly US$1.19 billion (about NT$36.9 billion) for the first half, down 31% year on year. Two other figures the same week point the same direction: technology provider DFNN's gaming commission revenue fell 67% in the first half, and the Bureau of Internal Revenue filed a PHP 2 billion tax claim against Hongsheng, a company linked to the Alice Guo case.


Bottom line: this report explains what has actually driven the Philippine contraction over the past year. Demand did not collapse; the regulator deliberately raised the minimum cost of staying in the market. A small operator that used to survive on PHP 10 million a month now owes PHP 9 million in fees regardless, which means the rules themselves remove it. -EV for sub-scale licensed online operators; +EV for mid-size and large operators that can absorb the MGF and pick up the vacated share. In practice: the list of viable Philippine partners will get materially shorter over the next 6 to 12 months, so partner selection cannot stop at whether a counterparty holds a licence — it has to check whether monthly gross gaming revenue actually clears the benchmark. Anyone below it will exit when the floor rises again in January 2027. For outside capital looking to buy into the market, Arden chief executive Tonet Quiogue's line about not building the structure first and disclosing it later is worth keeping: deals of this type in the Philippines usually fail not on price but because the ownership structure filed with PAGCOR does not match how the business actually runs. SIGNAL 03 · NORTH AMERICA · REGULATION

The prediction-market fight reaches the regulator's table: CME and Kalshi clash on the record at the CFTC Innovation Advisory Committee's first meeting; Korea and Denmark block Polymarket the same week

The reconstituted Innovation Advisory Committee of the CFTC (the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission) met for the first time on 20 August, with the chief executives of Coinbase, Kalshi (the CFTC-licensed US prediction market), Ripple and Polymarket (an offshore prediction market settling in crypto) at the table alongside senior figures from CME Group (the Chicago Mercantile Exchange) and Nasdaq. The exchange came between CME chairman Terry Duffy and Kalshi co-founder Luana Lopes Lara: Duffy argued federal regulators are allowing manipulable event contracts to reach retail consumers; after Lara pushed back, Duffy replied that CME's compliance department alone employs more people than Kalshi has in the entire company. The scale numbers were laid out too: Kalshi and Polymarket together traded roughly US$44.8 billion (about NT$1.39 trillion) in June, against average monthly handle of roughly US$14 billion across all legal US sports betting in 2025. Internationally, two blocks landed the same week: the Korea Communications Standards Commission (KCSC) voted on 18 August to block Polymarket, citing the winner-take-all structure as encouraging speculation; and the Danish Gambling Authority published a list of 98 unlicensed sites to be blocked on 19 August, with Polymarket on it, on the basis that these sites use Danish language, Danish kroner and marketing aimed at local users.


Bottom line: the US thread has no near-term resolution, but the international thread produced a clear answer this week — outside the US, mature regulated markets are treating prediction markets as unlicensed online casinos rather than financial derivatives. Korea and Denmark both used existing gambling law and existing blocking procedures; neither needed new legislation. That is materially bad news for prediction markets expanding internationally and good news for operators holding local licences. The rating stays Neutral only because the US federal preemption question is unresolved. In practice: two questions are enough to predict how any market will treat a prediction platform — whether the local regulator already has a site-blocking procedure it can reuse, and whether the platform is localising (local language, local currency, local marketing). Localised platforms are almost always handled as unlicensed operators; those three tests are exactly what Denmark based its determination on. SIGNAL 04 · LATAM · BRAZIL

Lula convenes an emergency ministerial meeting on the future of sports betting, saying "if it were up to me I would shut the bets down"; Minas Gerais bans ads in public spaces the same week

Brazilian president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva called ministers to the presidential residence at short notice this week to discuss whether the sports betting sector should continue. Attendees were notified only that morning. Defending a prohibitionist position, Lula said that if the decision were his, he would shut the bets down. That follows his instruction in late July for Workers' Party ministers to back PL 2.258/2026, the bill banning online casino, which the government wants through congress before the October election. In the same week the Minas Gerais state government issued a decree banning betting companies from advertising in state-owned public spaces, covering the Mineirão stadium, the Mineirinho arena, bus terminals, airports and state highways, and covering big screens, banners, stickers and audio ads; state concessionaires must terminate existing contracts with betting companies. For context: Brazil's regulated fixed-odds market handled BRL 410.85 billion in the first half (about NT$2.34 trillion), with gross gaming revenue of BRL 20.07 billion (about NT$114.4 billion), up 15.3% year on year — though June delivered only BRL 3.34 billion despite the World Cup, below market expectations for a tournament month.


Bottom line: Brazil produced two developments this week — one a statement of political intent at federal level, one an administrative order at state level — pointing the same way, with the state one already in force. Whether the president's position becomes law depends on the October election and the composition of congress, which carries a lot of uncertainty; the state decree carries none, because it executes on signature and ad placements come down the same day. -EV for online casino businesses in Brazil, since PL 2.258 targets exactly that; the effect on sports-betting-only brands is smaller, because the bill is designed to ban casino while leaving sports betting in place. In practice: Brazil planning this quarter should split into two tracks — treat online casino as potentially banned within 12 months and prepare an exit or conversion plan, and run sports betting on the assumption that it stays open with steadily shrinking ad inventory. The Minas Gerais model is easy for other states to copy, because it needs no legislation, only a state decree, and it targets stadiums and transport hubs, the most expensive physical placements there are. SIGNAL 05 · EUROPE · UK

The Premier League opened on 21 August with no gambling brand on any shirt front for the first time in 20 years; finance took five clubs, three still have no deal

The Premier League's 2026-27 season kicked off on 21 August with none of the 20 clubs carrying a gambling brand on the front of the shirt. The ban was voted through by the clubs themselves in 2023 with a three-year transition, which expires this season. The scope is narrow: only the shirt front is covered. Gambling brands may still appear on sleeves, training kit, pitchside boards and other club partnerships, provided they meet advertising and licensing rules. Eight clubs needed replacement sponsors this season: Aston Villa, Bournemouth, Brentford, Crystal Palace, Everton, Fulham, Nottingham Forest and Sunderland. At kickoff, Nottingham Forest, Sunderland and Chelsea still had no confirmed front-of-shirt sponsor. Among the categories moving in, finance is the largest this season, sponsoring 5 of the 20 clubs. Everton replaced Stake with the financial trading platform CMC Markets. Technology arrived too: Crystal Palace signed the application development platform Temporal, and Fulham signed the data technology company ClickHouse.


Bottom line: the value of watching this is not the Premier League itself but the sequence it demonstrates — the industry restricts itself first, and regulation follows. The clubs voted the front-of-shirt ban through in 2023, and three years later the UK government is tightening retail rules and online taxes; the two reinforce each other. -EV for gambling brands whose marketing is built on Premier League exposure: the most expensive and most recognisable placement is gone, and what remains — sleeves and pitchside boards — is cheaper and far less memorable. In practice: finance and technology buyers taking the vacated shirt fronts show that the buyer mix for sports sponsorship is changing, which will weaken bargaining position on other placements in future club negotiations, because clubs now have alternative bidders. If the same pressure appears in the Philippines or Brazil, it will most likely arrive first as a league or club self-imposed rule rather than as law, so sports sponsorship contracts should keep a clause allowing placement to be renegotiated if the rights holder changes its rules. Regulation−Negative🆕 Asia · Philippines
ASIA · PHILIPPINES

Philippine Senate Bill 2347 advanced this week, leaving operators with only their own site and app

The Manila Times · Asia Gaming Brief · 08/17–08/19

Senate Bill 2347, the gambling advertising ban filed by Senate President Francis Escudero, gained visible momentum in congress and in the press this week. The draft cuts licensed gambling operators' advertising channels down to their own website and their own app: television, radio, print, online platforms and social media are all prohibited; celebrity endorsement and influencer marketing are prohibited; and any permitted consumer communication must carry a responsible gambling notice. Penalties cover operators, advertisers, media companies, platforms and the responsible officers of the businesses involved, with a first offence carrying up to PHP 200,000 (about NT$110,000) or up to one year in prison, a second offence PHP 300,000 or two years, and a third or subsequent offence PHP 500,000 or three years. The bill allows a one-year transition period.


Bottom line: this draft would hit acquisition harder than PAGCOR's minimum guaranteed fee does — the MGF raises the cost of staying in the market, while an advertising ban removes the means of bringing players in. The real force sits in the penalties reaching media companies and platforms: once it passes, local media and agencies will pull back on their own, so practical enforcement will start well before the statute bites. -EV for brands whose revenue depends primarily on the Philippines. In practice: the first step is to quantify what share of current acquisition comes from channels this draft would close, and treat that share as the exposure. What remains is the owned website, the owned app, and formats that do not count as advertising — user referral, CRM against existing lists, and content that carries no gambling brand. All three take more than six months to build, which lines up with the one-year transition if work starts now.

NORTH AMERICA · US

US operators adopt a self-written advertising code: no campus placement, no framing gambling as a way to make money

CDC Gaming · 08/18

The Responsible Online Gaming Association (ROGA) published marketing and advertising standards on 18 August, adopted by all members. Members include Bally's, bet365, BetMGM, DraftKings, Fanatics Betting & Gaming, FanDuel, Hard Rock Digital and Penn Entertainment. The code covers three areas: marketing only to those of legal age, accuracy and transparency in content, and responsible gambling. Specific provisions include a ban on advertising on college campuses or targeting college students, a ban on partnerships with amateur college athletes, and a ban on language describing gambling as a financial strategy or an easy way to make money.


Bottom line: this is self-regulation with no legal force, but its practical effect is to draw a line that legislators can later copy wholesale. The Premier League front-of-shirt ban took the same route (see Signal 05). +EV for brands already producing creative to this standard; -EV for brands that drive conversion with copy about guaranteed wins or easy doubling. In practice: the clause on not describing gambling as a financial strategy belongs directly in an internal creative review checklist, because the test is easy to apply — if copy contains words such as invest, yield, return or double, replace it. Equivalent language exists in the Australian, UK and Brazilian rules, which makes this a minimum standard that holds across every market.

EDITOR'S PICK

Four markets changed advertising rules this week, using four different instruments

📍 Editorial synthesis from this issue's Signal 01, Signal 05 and multiple §1 sources — no single source link

The difference in instruments is worth recording on its own. Australia used an act of parliament, drafted in fine detail with explicit penalties, effective January 2027 (Signal 01). The Premier League used an internal club vote with no legal force, but because every club voted for it, the practical constraint is equivalent to a rule (Signal 05). The Philippines is using a congressional bill still under review, yet local media have already started restricting themselves. The US used industry self-regulation, with eight ROGA members signing on and nobody compelled. Minas Gerais used a state administrative decree, the fastest of the four, in force on signature (Signal 04).


Bottom line: what these instruments share is the list of things they regulate — celebrity and influencer endorsement, exposure during live sport, how inducements are delivered, and copy that presents gambling as a way to make money. Only speed and enforceability differ. That tells operators the tightening of advertising rules is not a local quirk of one market but the direction the industry is moving as a whole, with each jurisdiction picking whichever route it can execute fastest. In practice: rather than tracking legislative progress market by market, invert the exercise — build a single list of what these four markets have already banned, assume every market will ban the same items within 24 months, and sequence replacement work against that list. The cost of doing this is abandoning some channels that still work; the cost of not doing it is a full stop on acquisition in the week local rules land. The first is plannable, the second is not.
